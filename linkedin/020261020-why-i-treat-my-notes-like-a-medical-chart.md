# Why I Treat My Notes Like a Medical Chart (and Why We Should Reread Our Notes)

**Date:** 02026-10-20
**Original Post:** [LinkedIn](https://lnkd.in/p/g8xdHgTP)

A few years ago, I was in a department meeting surrounded by colleagues across all levels, from individual contributors to senior leadership. We were discussing performance evaluations when someone asked a surprisingly candid question: "Does anyone here actually know how to take good notes on people?"

Silence. No one in the room, manager or otherwise, had a confident answer.

That moment stuck with me. Up until then, I had been treating my daily notes like an academic exercise, an abstract file created to prepare for a test on a specific topic later on. But that room full of silent peers made me realize something fundamental: I am human, my colleagues are human, and my notes are a record of my actual lived experiences with people, not just sterile text dumps. In software development, we are often writing code to solve problems for people.

To solve that problem, I shifted my mental model and started treating my daily notes like a medical chart.

Instead of appending to a sprawling, endless running log, I treat each day like a physician logging a chart. I start at the top of the document with a date stamp, capturing meeting notes, human interactions, and key decisions in a structured ledger. That simple shift turned scattered text into a clean, searchable timeline spanning months and years.

Another breakthrough came when I learned about Benjamin Franklin's note-taking habits. I realized I was still using less than half the power of my notes. Like most people, I was treating my notes like a passive storage bucket or a search index to query when I was stuck. Franklin did not just store notes; he regularly reread them.

When you build a ritual of rereading past notes, something remarkable happens. You spot patterns you missed in the heat of the moment. You reconnect disparate ideas, track your growth with people and projects, and bring forgotten priorities back to the surface before they slip away. Notes stop being a passive slate for thoughts and become an active tool for strategic thinking and human connection.

How do you interact with your past notes? Are they a static archive, or do you have a ritual for bringing old insights back into your active work?
